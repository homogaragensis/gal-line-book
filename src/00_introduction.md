# Introduction

This book documents the open development of the video game **GAL-LINE** in a tutorial format. 
The sections of the book will be published gradually as development progresses, 
and the code snippets appearing in the text will be linked to specific commits on GitHub. 
This will allow future readers to view the history of the repository for as long as GitHub exists, 
and to see through the commit history how development actually took place.

This project has various purposes; certainly the first is to show what happens when you develop 
an idea all the way through, no matter how simple, 
and how iterations can lead to achieving a commercial result. 
But no less important is the mission to showcase the idea of *"simple"* software that our 
studio aims to promote. By simple software, we mean software that tries as much as possible 
not to complicate the nature of the problem. Instead, it is written by attempting to clearly 
define the limits, relevant measurements, and the inputs and outputs of the system 
even before development begins. Simple software is written trying to minimize 
its power consumption, even at the expense of the hours of work spent on it.

I myself spent years trying to deconstruct the best practices drilled into us by universities and 
books, because they serve industrial competitiveness, and in a way GAL-LINE 
is the first product I am trying to complete by fully following this philosophy.

The game will be developed using JavaScript, HTML, and CSS. 
To be able to follow the book, a basic knowledge of these programming languages will be necessary, 
but not necessarily prior experience with video game development. 
If necessary, some other scripting language might appear to develop tools for the art pipeline, 
the development of which I consider an integral part of the game.

The choice of Javascript is not at all because I like it. But Javscript is still C-like,
so the most known style of programming language, and I love how easy it is to
get started coding something in the browser environment for anyone intrested in trying.

I believe the concepts and approaches presented in the book have a lot less to do 
with the language than what they have to do with a solid understanding of the problems 
faced while programming. 
In fact one that has a solid understanding of imperative programming should be able to grasp
all of what is described in this book, even if he has never seen a computer.

But let's talk about GAL-LINE. 
The idea for the game was born in a garage, looking for surreal scenarios for a side-scrolling 
arcade. Basically, there is a chicken running past the yellow line of the station, 
encountering legs in its path, and striking them to clear a way. 
The game's name is a bilingual pun, combining the Italian word "Gallina" (which means chicken) 
and the English word "line," alluding to the yellow line. 
The essence of the game is adrenaline, 
and its replay value comes from the vast assortment of improbable characters 
you find yourself controlling and facing.

I hope this book won't fail to satisfy at least one of the big objectives I announced so bravely.
